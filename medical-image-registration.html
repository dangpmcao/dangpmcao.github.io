Medical Image Registration: Geometry, Warping, and Evaluation

A mathematical route from physical coordinates to deformation fields—and from a visually plausible alignment to evidence that the registration is actually trustworthy.

01 / DEFINITIONWhat is medical image registration?

Medical image registration estimates a spatial correspondence between two or more images. One image is designated the fixed image \(F\), and another the moving image \(M\). Registration seeks a transformation \(\phi\) that maps locations in the fixed-image domain \(\Omega_F\) to anatomically corresponding locations in the moving-image domain \(\Omega_M\):

\[ \phi : \Omega_F \subset \mathbb{R}^{d} \longrightarrow \Omega_M \subset \mathbb{R}^{d}, \qquad \mathbf{x} \longmapsto \phi(\mathbf{x}). \]

The registered—or warped—moving image is therefore the composition

\[ (M \circ \phi)(\mathbf{x}) = M\!\left(\phi(\mathbf{x})\right). \]
The central goal: after registration, \(F(\mathbf{x})\) and \(M(\phi(\mathbf{x}))\) should describe the same anatomical location, even if the scans differ in time, viewpoint, patient, or imaging modality.

This correspondence enables longitudinal disease monitoring, multimodal fusion such as CT–MRI or MR–ultrasound, motion estimation, atlas construction, population analysis, image-guided intervention, and label propagation. The required transformation depends on the application: aligning two rigid bones is different from estimating respiratory motion or inter-patient brain morphology.

02 / FORMULATIONRegistration as an inverse problem

A standard intensity-based method chooses \(\phi\) from an admissible transformation space \(\Phi\) by minimizing an energy with a data term and a regularizer:

\[ \phi^{\star} = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\phi \in \Phi} \underbrace{\mathcal{D}\!\left(F, M \circ \phi\right)}_{\text{image dissimilarity}} + \lambda \underbrace{\mathcal{R}(\phi)}_{\text{deformation prior}}. \tag{1} \]

The data term asks whether the aligned images agree; the regularizer asks whether the transformation is plausible. The weight \(\lambda\) expresses a real trade-off. A weak prior may fit noise or artifacts, while an overly strong prior may erase true anatomical motion.

Feature-based registration replaces dense image comparison with corresponding structures. For paired landmarks \(\{(\mathbf{p}_i,\mathbf{q}_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}\), for example, a natural objective is

\[ \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{landmark}}(\phi) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left\|\phi(\mathbf{p}_i)-\mathbf{q}_i\right\|_2^2. \]

Modern learning-based systems may predict \(\phi\) in one network pass, but the geometric meaning of the output and the evaluation requirements remain the same.

03 / IMAGE GEOMETRYFrom voxel indices to physical space

Continuous anatomy, discrete measurements

An idealized \(d\)-dimensional medical image is a continuous function \(I:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{c}\), where \(c=1\) for a scalar image and \(c>1\) for multichannel data. A scanner stores samples on a finite lattice:

\[ I[\mathbf{n}], \qquad \mathbf{n}=(n_1,\ldots,n_d)^\top, \quad 0 \le n_k < N_k. \]

The array index \(\mathbf{n}\) is not itself a physical location. Medical images carry metadata describing the origin \(\mathbf{o}\), voxel spacing \(\mathbf{s}\), and orientation matrix \(D\). With \(S=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\ldots,s_d)\), the index-to-world mapping is

\[ \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{world}} = \mathbf{o} + D S\mathbf{n}. \tag{2} \] \[ \mathbf{n} = S^{-1}D^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{world}}-\mathbf{o}). \tag{3} \]

For an orthonormal direction matrix, \(D^{-1}=D^\top\). Equations (2)–(3) explain why two arrays of equal shape are not necessarily aligned: they may have different spacing, origins, or anatomical axes. Ignoring this metadata silently changes the physical problem.

Homogeneous coordinates

Translation is not linear in ordinary coordinates. By appending a \(1\), affine mappings become a single matrix multiplication:

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf{x}}= \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \widetilde{\mathbf{x}}' = \underbrace{ \begin{bmatrix} A & \mathbf{t}\\ \mathbf{0}^{\top} & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{T} \widetilde{\mathbf{x}}. \tag{4} \]

This representation also makes coordinate conversion explicit. If \(W_F\) and \(W_M\) map fixed and moving voxel indices into world coordinates, and \(T_W\) is defined in world space, the fixed-index to moving-index map is

\[ T_{F\rightarrow M}^{\mathrm{index}} = W_M^{-1}\,T_W\,W_F. \tag{5} \]

The order matters: matrix composition reads from right to left. Equation (5) first enters physical space, applies the physical transform, and then returns to the moving-image lattice.

Normalized coordinates

Many deep-learning samplers use coordinates normalized to \([-1,1]^d\). Under the common corner-aligned convention, an index \(n_k\in[0,N_k-1]\) becomes

\[ \xi_k = 2\frac{n_k}{N_k-1}-1, \qquad n_k = \frac{N_k-1}{2}(\xi_k+1). \]

Libraries differ in whether normalized endpoints refer to voxel centers or image corners. A half-voxel convention mismatch can introduce a systematic registration error even when the learned deformation is otherwise correct.

04 / TRANSFORMATIONSGlobal and local motion models

A transformation model determines which kinds of anatomical change the registration is allowed to express. This choice is not merely computational: it is an assumption about the anatomy. If the model is too restrictive, real motion remains unexplained. If it is too flexible, the optimizer may manufacture implausible correspondences that improve image similarity.

Translation

Changes position only. Shape, orientation, distance, and volume are preserved.

Rigid

Combines translation and rotation. Appropriate when the object should not deform.

Affine

Adds global scaling and shear. Straight lines remain straight, but shape may change.

Deformable

Allows position-dependent motion. Different anatomical regions can move differently.

Translation: the simplest motion

A translation moves every point by the same vector \(\mathbf{t}\). In \(d\) dimensions,

\[ \phi_{\mathrm{trans}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t}, \qquad \mathbf{t}=(t_1,\ldots,t_d)^\top. \]

For a 2-D image, \(\mathbf{t}=(t_x,t_y)^\top\); for a 3-D volume, \(\mathbf{t}=(t_x,t_y,t_z)^\top\). The same displacement is applied at every location, so the vector between any two transformed points is unchanged:

\[ \phi(\mathbf{x}_1)-\phi(\mathbf{x}_2) =(\mathbf{x}_1+\mathbf{t})-(\mathbf{x}_2+\mathbf{t}) =\mathbf{x}_1-\mathbf{x}_2. \]

This equality explains why translation preserves distances, angles, shape, and volume. Its inverse is simply \(\phi^{-1}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{t}\). Translation is often estimated first—for example, by aligning image centers or centers of mass—because a large initial offset can prevent later optimization from finding the correct overlap.

The units of \(\mathbf{t}\) must be explicit. A shift of three voxels is not a shift of three millimeters unless the voxel spacing is exactly \(1\,\mathrm{mm}\). For anisotropic volumes, the same index displacement can represent different physical distances along different axes.

Rotation: changing orientation

A rotation changes orientation while preserving lengths and handedness. In 2-D, a counterclockwise rotation by angle \(\theta\) about the origin is

\[ R(\theta)= \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \phi_{\mathrm{rot}}(\mathbf{x})=R(\theta)\mathbf{x}. \]

The constraints \(R^\top R=I\) and \(\det R=1\) are what make \(R\) a proper rotation. Orthogonality gives \(\|R\mathbf{x}\|_2=\|\mathbf{x}\|_2\), while the positive determinant excludes reflections. The inverse is especially simple: \(R^{-1}=R^\top=R(-\theta)\).

Three-dimensional rotation has three degrees of freedom. Rotations about the coordinate axes can be written as

\[ R_x(\alpha)= \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\ 0&\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha\\ 0&\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha \end{bmatrix}, \quad R_y(\beta)= \begin{bmatrix} \cos\beta&0&\sin\beta\\ 0&1&0\\ -\sin\beta&0&\cos\beta \end{bmatrix}, \] \[ R_z(\gamma)= \begin{bmatrix} \cos\gamma&-\sin\gamma&0\\ \sin\gamma&\cos\gamma&0\\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

A composite Euler rotation might be defined as \(R=R_z(\gamma)R_y(\beta)R_x(\alpha)\). The convention must be documented because 3-D rotations do not commute: in general, \(R_xR_y\ne R_yR_x\). Changing the multiplication order changes the final orientation.

The center of rotation

Applying \(R\mathbf{x}\) rotates about the coordinate origin, which is often a corner or scanner-defined point rather than the center of the anatomy. To rotate about a chosen center \(\mathbf{c}\), first move that center to the origin, rotate, and move it back:

\[ \phi_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathbf{x}) =R(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{c})+\mathbf{c} =R\mathbf{x}+(I-R)\mathbf{c}. \]

This is why initialization matters. Even with the correct angle, rotating about the wrong center produces a translation-like error whose magnitude grows with distance from the center.

Rigid transformation: rotation plus translation

A rigid transformation combines the two motions while preserving the Euclidean distance between every pair of points:

\[ \phi(\mathbf{x})=R(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{c})+\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{t}, \qquad R^\top R=I,\quad \det R=1. \tag{6} \]

It has three degrees of freedom in 2-D—one angle and two translations—and six in 3-D—three rotational and three translational parameters. For any \(\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_2\),

\[ \|\phi(\mathbf{x}_1)-\phi(\mathbf{x}_2)\|_2 =\|R(\mathbf{x}_1-\mathbf{x}_2)\|_2 =\|\mathbf{x}_1-\mathbf{x}_2\|_2. \]

Rigid registration is a sensible model for the skull, bones, surgical instruments, or the initial alignment of two scans. It is not sufficient for soft-tissue deformation, respiration, cardiac motion, or inter-subject anatomical variation.

Similarity and affine transformations

A similarity transform adds one uniform scale \(s>0\) to rigid motion, \(\phi(\mathbf{x})=sR\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t}\). It preserves angles and relative shape but changes every length by the same factor.

An affine model is more general. It permits rotation, translation, anisotropic scale, and shear:

\[ \phi(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t}, \qquad A\in GL(d). \tag{7} \]

The matrix \(A\) can be understood as a composition of simpler linear operations. A useful conceptual factorization is \(A=RSH\), where \(R\) rotates, \(S\) scales along selected axes, and \(H\) shears. The factorization is convention-dependent, but it emphasizes that an arbitrary affine matrix mixes several physical effects.

Unlike a rigid map, an affine map need not preserve lengths, angles, or volume. Parallel lines remain parallel, and its local volume ratio is constant everywhere: \(\lvert\det A\rvert\). Thus \(\lvert\det A\rvert=1\) preserves global volume, a value greater than one expands it, and a value between zero and one contracts it. A negative determinant also includes a reflection, which is usually anatomically inappropriate.

Why deformable transformations are different

Every global model above applies one parameter set to the entire image. Soft anatomy does not behave that way: the heart wall contracts locally, lungs slide against the pleura, and inter-patient organs differ by more than a single global scale. A deformable—or nonrigid—transformation therefore allows the motion to vary with position.

The most direct representation is an identity map plus a displacement field \(\mathbf{u}\):

\[ \phi(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}). \tag{8} \]

At each point \(\mathbf{x}\), the vector \(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\) specifies how far and in which direction that point is mapped. A constant displacement field reduces to translation. A field that varies linearly can reproduce affine motion. A genuinely nonlinear field lets neighboring regions move by different amounts.

Dense displacement fields

A dense field stores a \(d\)-component vector at every voxel. It can represent fine local motion and is the common output of convolutional registration networks. Its flexibility is also its weakness: neighboring vectors can change abruptly unless the model, loss, or parameterization enforces plausible spatial behavior.

A typical smoothness prior penalizes spatial derivatives, but smoothness alone does not guarantee invertibility. A field may look visually smooth while still folding space where its Jacobian determinant becomes non-positive.

B-spline free-form deformations

A parametric free-form deformation stores coefficients on a coarser control-point lattice and interpolates the dense displacement between them. For a tensor-product B-spline basis \(B_r\),

\[ \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{k}} \prod_{j=1}^{d} B_{r}\!\left(\frac{x_j}{h_j}-k_j\right), \tag{9} \]

Here \(h_j\) is the control-point spacing, \(r\) is the spline degree, and each coefficient \(\mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{k}}\) moves one control point. B-splines have local support: changing one coefficient influences only a neighborhood rather than the entire image. This makes optimization efficient and gives direct control over deformation scale.

Control-point spacing acts as an implicit regularizer. A coarse lattice produces broad, smooth motion but may miss small structures. A fine lattice captures local detail but introduces more degrees of freedom and a greater risk of fitting noise. Multiresolution methods often begin with a coarse lattice and progressively refine it.

Diffeomorphic transformation

A displacement field does not automatically define a one-to-one map. If two regions map to the same location or if the field folds, anatomical correspondence becomes ambiguous. A diffeomorphism addresses this by requiring the transformation to be differentiable, bijective, and to have a differentiable inverse.

One construction treats deformation as continuous motion generated by a time-varying velocity field \(\mathbf{v}_t\):

\[ \frac{\partial \phi_t(\mathbf{x})}{\partial t} = \mathbf{v}_t\!\left(\phi_t(\mathbf{x})\right), \qquad \phi_0(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}, \qquad \phi=\phi_1. \tag{10} \]

The point starts at \(\mathbf{x}\) when \(t=0\) and follows the velocity field until \(t=1\). Under suitable smoothness conditions, the intermediate maps remain invertible, so topology is preserved throughout the flow. If the velocity is constant in time, \(\mathbf{v}_t=\mathbf{v}\), the final transform is often written \(\phi=\exp(\mathbf{v})\) and can be approximated numerically using scaling and squaring.

Combining global and local alignment

Deformable registration is rarely expected to solve a large initial misalignment by itself. A common pipeline estimates a rigid or affine map first and then refines it with a local deformation. If \(T_{\mathrm{global}}\) is the initial map and \(\phi_{\mathrm{local}}\) is the refinement, the composite map is

\[ \phi_{\mathrm{total}} =\phi_{\mathrm{local}}\circ T_{\mathrm{global}}, \qquad \phi_{\mathrm{total}}(\mathbf{x}) =\phi_{\mathrm{local}}\!\left(T_{\mathrm{global}}(\mathbf{x})\right). \]

The composition order must match the coordinate convention and implementation. Writing the order explicitly is one of the simplest ways to prevent a registration pipeline from applying a correct transformation in the wrong space.

05 / IMAGE WARPINGInterpolation, forward maps, and backward maps

Why interpolation is necessary

A transformed point almost never lands exactly on a voxel center. To evaluate \(M(\mathbf{y})\) at a continuous location \(\mathbf{y}\), we reconstruct an interpolant from discrete samples:

\[ \widehat{M}(\mathbf{y}) = \sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}} M[\mathbf{n}]\,K(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{n}), \tag{11} \]

where \(K\) is an interpolation kernel. Nearest-neighbor interpolation chooses the closest sample and is appropriate for categorical labels. Linear interpolation is usually preferable for scalar intensities. In \(d\) dimensions, multilinear interpolation is

\[ \widehat{M}(\mathbf{y}) = \sum_{\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{d}} M[\lfloor\mathbf{y}\rfloor+\mathbf{b}] \prod_{k=1}^{d} \big[b_k\alpha_k+(1-b_k)(1-\alpha_k)\big], \] \[ \boldsymbol{\alpha}=\mathbf{y}-\lfloor\mathbf{y}\rfloor. \tag{12} \]

Equation (12) gives bilinear interpolation in 2-D and trilinear interpolation in 3-D. Higher-order B-spline or sinc-like kernels may produce smoother results, but they are more expensive and can overshoot near sharp boundaries.

Images and labels require different treatment. Linear interpolation of a segmentation turns class IDs into meaningless fractional values. Warp label maps with nearest-neighbor interpolation, or interpolate one-hot class probabilities and take an argmax afterward.

Forward mapping: push

A forward warp starts from each moving voxel and pushes its value to a transformed location:

\[ \mathbf{y}=\psi(\mathbf{x}), \qquad M[\mathbf{x}] \longrightarrow I_{\mathrm{out}}[\mathbf{y}]. \]

Because transformed samples are irregularly spaced, several sources may collide and some output voxels may receive nothing. Forward warping therefore needs splatting, accumulation rules, and hole filling.

Backward mapping: pull

A backward warp visits every output location \(\mathbf{y}\) and asks where its value came from:

\[ I_{\mathrm{out}}(\mathbf{y}) = \widehat{M}\!\left(\psi^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\right). \tag{13} \]

Registration is often defined directly as a fixed-to-moving sampling map \(\phi\), in which case

\[ M_{\mathrm{warped}}(\mathbf{x}) = \widehat{M}\!\left(\phi(\mathbf{x})\right), \qquad \mathbf{x}\in\Omega_F. \tag{14} \]

This is the usual implementation because every fixed-grid voxel receives one sampled value. A common source of mistakes is confusing the conceptual direction of anatomical motion with the direction of the sampling map.

Resample once

Every interpolation modifies the signal. If several transforms are required, compose their coordinate maps first and resample the original image once:

\[ M \circ \phi_1 \circ \phi_2 \quad\text{in one sampling operation, rather than}\quad (M\circ\phi_1)\circ\phi_2. \]

This reduces cumulative blurring and preserves quantitative intensity information.

06 / EVALUATIONHow do we know the registration is correct?

A low training objective is not proof of anatomical correctness. Evaluation should be independent of the features or similarity term used to optimize the registration, and it should measure errors that matter for the intended application.

Anatomical landmarks and corresponding points

Suppose experts mark corresponding points \(\mathbf{p}_i\) in the fixed image and \(\mathbf{q}_i\) in the moving image. The target registration error (TRE) is

\[ \operatorname{TRE}_i = \left\|\phi(\mathbf{p}_i)-\mathbf{q}_i\right\|_2, \qquad \operatorname{mTRE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{TRE}_i. \tag{15} \]

Report the distribution—not only its mean. The median, standard deviation, 95th percentile, and maximum expose catastrophic local failures. Coordinates must be expressed in physical units, usually millimeters.

Landmarks should cover the volume and the clinically relevant regions. Sparse or easily identifiable points may overstate performance elsewhere. Inter-observer annotation variability supplies a useful lower bound on interpretable TRE.

Independent image similarity

For monomodal images, mean squared error and normalized cross-correlation (NCC) are common:

\[ \operatorname{MSE}(F,M_\phi) = \frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega} \left(F(\mathbf{x})-M_\phi(\mathbf{x})\right)^2, \] \[ \operatorname{NCC}(F,M_\phi) = \frac{\sum_{\mathbf{x}}(F(\mathbf{x})-\bar F)(M_\phi(\mathbf{x})-\bar M_\phi)} {\sqrt{\sum_{\mathbf{x}}(F(\mathbf{x})-\bar F)^2} \sqrt{\sum_{\mathbf{x}}(M_\phi(\mathbf{x})-\bar M_\phi)^2}}. \tag{16} \]

For multimodal images, mutual information measures statistical dependence. From a joint intensity distribution \(p_{FM}\),

\[ \operatorname{MI}(F,M_\phi) = \sum_{f,m}p_{FM}(f,m) \log\frac{p_{FM}(f,m)}{p_F(f)p_M(m)}. \tag{17} \]

If NCC, MI, or another measure was optimized during registration, reporting the same measure as the sole evaluation is circular. Use a held-out metric, landmarks, labels, or downstream task performance.

Segmentation-label overlap

Let \(A\) be a fixed-image anatomical label and \(B_\phi\) the corresponding warped moving label. The Dice similarity coefficient and Jaccard index are

\[ \operatorname{Dice}(A,B_\phi) = \frac{2|A\cap B_\phi|}{|A|+|B_\phi|}, \qquad \operatorname{Jaccard}(A,B_\phi) = \frac{|A\cap B_\phi|}{|A\cup B_\phi|}. \tag{18} \]

Overlap is easy to interpret but size-dependent: a small displacement can severely reduce Dice for a tiny structure while barely affecting a large organ. Boundary metrics complement it. With surface point sets \(S_A\) and \(S_B\), the symmetric average surface distance is

\[ \operatorname{ASSD}(S_A,S_B) = \frac{ \sum_{a\in S_A}\min_{b\in S_B}\|a-b\|_2 +\sum_{b\in S_B}\min_{a\in S_A}\|b-a\|_2} {|S_A|+|S_B|}. \tag{19} \]

The Hausdorff distance measures the worst boundary disagreement, although a robust 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance is usually less sensitive to a single mislabeled voxel.

Deformation-field quality

Good overlap can coexist with an implausible deformation. The field itself should therefore be audited using inverse consistency, derivative-based smoothness, Jacobian determinants, folding counts, and displacement magnitudes. These quantities are developed next.

07 / DEFORMATION QUALITYConsistency, smoothness, volume, and topology

Consistency

If \(\phi\) maps fixed to moving and \(\psi\) maps moving to fixed, ideal inverse consistency requires

\[ \psi\circ\phi=\operatorname{Id}_{\Omega_F}, \qquad \phi\circ\psi=\operatorname{Id}_{\Omega_M}. \]

A measurable inverse-consistency error is

\[ \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{IC}} = \frac{1}{|\Omega_F|} \sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_F} \left\|\psi(\phi(\mathbf{x}))-\mathbf{x}\right\|_2^2. \tag{20} \]

Cycle consistency can be extended across multiple images or time points. For transformations \(\phi_{A\to B}\), \(\phi_{B\to C}\), and \(\phi_{C\to A}\), their composition should approximate the identity. Temporal consistency is especially important for cardiac and respiratory sequences.

Smoothness

For \(\phi(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\), diffusion regularization penalizes first derivatives:

\[ \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{diff}}(\mathbf{u}) = \int_{\Omega}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\|_F^2\,d\mathbf{x}. \tag{21} \]

Bending energy penalizes curvature through second derivatives:

\[ \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{bend}}(\mathbf{u}) = \int_{\Omega} \sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d} \left(\frac{\partial^2 u_i} {\partial x_j\partial x_k}\right)^2 d\mathbf{x}. \tag{22} \]

These are priors, not universal truths. Sliding organs can have discontinuous tangential motion at interfaces; forcing global smoothness may be anatomically wrong. Smoothness must be interpreted relative to tissue mechanics and image resolution.

Volume preservation and loss

The deformation gradient and its Jacobian determinant are

\[ J_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla\phi(\mathbf{x}) = I+\nabla\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}), \qquad j(\mathbf{x})=\det J_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}). \tag{23} \]

The determinant describes local volume change:

  • \(j(\mathbf{x})=1\): local volume is preserved.
  • \(0<j(\mathbf{x})<1\): local contraction.
  • \(j(\mathbf{x})>1\): local expansion.
  • \(j(\mathbf{x})\le 0\): singularity or orientation-reversing fold.

The change-of-variables formula makes the interpretation precise. For a region \(A\subset\Omega\),

\[ |\phi(A)|=\int_A \left|\det J_\phi(\mathbf{x})\right|d\mathbf{x}. \tag{24} \]

Useful summaries include the mean and percentiles of \(\log j\), the fraction of voxels with \(j\le0\), and spatial maps of extreme expansion or contraction. Whether \(j\approx1\) is desirable depends on the anatomy: incompressible tissue suggests volume preservation, while a filling ventricle or growing lesion does not.

Topology

A topologically valid deformation should not tear tissue, glue distinct regions together, or fold space. A smooth map with

\[ \det J_\phi(\mathbf{x})>0 \qquad \text{for every }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega \tag{25} \]

is locally orientation-preserving and locally invertible by the inverse function theorem. This is necessary for a diffeomorphism, though on its own it does not prove global one-to-one behavior. Boundary handling, global injectivity, and the numerical integration scheme also matter.

Topology can additionally refer to anatomical labels themselves. Connected-component counts, holes, and Euler characteristic can reveal whether a warped structure has acquired implausible breaks or handles even when its overlap score is high.

08 / PRACTICEA compact evaluation checklist

  1. State the map direction. Specify whether \(\phi\) maps fixed points to moving points or represents forward physical motion.
  2. Work in physical space. Respect origin, spacing, direction, and units; document any normalized-coordinate convention.
  3. Match interpolation to data. Use continuous interpolation for intensities and categorical interpolation for labels.
  4. Use independent accuracy measures. Combine landmarks, held-out image metrics, overlap, and boundary distance where possible.
  5. Inspect the full error distribution. Means hide regional and worst-case failures.
  6. Audit the deformation. Report inverse consistency, smoothness, Jacobian statistics, and folding rate.
  7. Interpret metrics anatomically. Neither perfect smoothness nor exact volume preservation is appropriate for every organ.
  8. Visualize. Inspect checkerboards, difference images, warped grids, landmark residuals, and Jacobian maps.
The main lesson: registration quality is multidimensional. Image agreement, anatomical correspondence, and transformation plausibility answer different questions; a reliable study evaluates all three.
